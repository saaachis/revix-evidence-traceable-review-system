How a score is worked out

In plain language, with the actual arithmetic shown. If you disagree with any step you can see exactly where to disagree, which is the point.

We collect the evidence

Owner reviews, expert reviews, forum threads, video transcripts and official records, read at a polite rate, cached, and linked back to. We store references and structure, not copies of anyone's writing.

We match every review to one exact variant

A petrol listing is never matched to a diesel variant, whatever the text similarity says. Specifications act as hard constraints before any learned matching runs, which is why the matching can be accurate on a small budget. Anything ambiguous goes to a human queue instead of being guessed.

We split each review into nine topics

A review is rarely about one thing. One paragraph can praise the ride and condemn the service centre, so we score those separately rather than averaging them into a single star rating.

We work out how much each review should count

This is the part that makes Revix different, and it is spelled out below.

We combine, and we say how sure we are

A weighted average, plus a range that widens when the evidence is thin or lopsided.

What decides a review's weight

Every review gets a weight between 0 and 1 for each topic. It is a product of six terms, so any single bad signal pulls the whole weight down:

weight = source_prior × (1 − spam_probability) × reliability × aspect_fit × recency_decay × launch_window_correction

Five of those are ordinary. aspect_fit is the interesting one, and it exists only because this is the automobile domain.

Why the same person is a good witness to one thing and a bad witness to another

An owner who has covered 500 km can tell you a great deal about the showroom, the delivery experience and the first service appointment. They can tell you nothing useful about whether the clutch survives 60,000 km. An owner at 60,000 km is the reverse.

Indian review platforms record ownership duration and kilometres driven, and then throw that information away by averaging every review equally. We use it. Credibility is therefore not one number per review; it is a short vector across topic groups:

{ "base": 0.71, "by_aspect_group": { "durability": 0.88, "immediate": 0.42, "service": 0.83, "efficiency": 0.79 } }

Why the range is not decoration

When reviews carry different weights, the number of reviews stops being a useful measure of how much you know. Two hundred low-weight reviews can carry less real information than thirty high-weight ones. The standard way to express that is the effective sample size:

n_eff = (Σ w)² / Σ w²

On the Creta page, 412 reviews produce an effective sample of 178. That is why flipping to credibility weighting makes the range wider, not narrower. Being more careful about which evidence counts means admitting you have less of it.

And we check that the range is honest rather than asserting it. Across every test run we measure whether the 80% range actually contains the answer 80% of the time, and publish the result on the metrics page.

Why we show disagreement instead of hiding it

When owners split on a topic we do not just report the split, we try to explain it. For each topic we test which characteristic best accounts for the disagreement: transmission, fuel type, model year, city, ownership length, or source. That produces statements like:

71% of the disagreement about the gearbox is explained by transmission type. Automatic owners rate it 6.2. Manual owners rate it 8.8.

That is a statistical decomposition, not a language model's opinion. It is cheaper, more reliable, and far more useful to somebody choosing between two variants of the same car.

What we do not claim

  • We do not claim our verdict is objectively correct. There is no ground truth for consumer opinion, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something.
  • We do not claim to detect every fake review. The spam score is one term in a product, not a filter that deletes things.
  • We do not rank by who pays us, because nobody pays us.
  • Where we do not have enough evidence, we show no score at all.

What we can test is whether trust weighting beats star averaging. We hold out long-term verified owners as a target, estimate that target from the remaining mixed-quality reviews, and compare the weighting strategies against it. Those results are public on the metrics page, including the cases where we do worse.
